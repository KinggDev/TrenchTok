The coin

market cap

What happens when a TikTok page about memecoins gets paid by a memecoin of its own.

Why this exists

Most edits about memecoins are made for free. A creator watches a chart run, cuts a clip, posts it, and hopes the coin's community notices. The views help the coin. Almost nothing comes back to the edit.

Some pages went the other way and took paid promos, then posted whatever the client wanted. Trenchtok is not a reply to any one of those. It starts from a plainer thought: if the edits are what bring people to the trenches, the edits should be funded in the open, by the coin that benefits from them.

So the page has a coin. It trades on Solana. People buy and sell it for their own reasons. A slice of every trade ends up in the edit budget, and the budget pays for the next edit. Nobody is promised anything by holding it.

The setup

The coin has one treasury wallet. Its address is public and listed on the record page. Every spend leaves from that wallet, so anyone can check where the money went against what the page says it did.

A deposit happens when enough creator fees have collected. Eight tenths go into the edit budget. Two tenths stay in the reserve. Then the page plans the week's edits, biggest spend first.

Each spend is posted with the amount, what it was for and the edit it went into. The edit goes up, the views come in, and the record is updated. That is the whole loop.

The vote

The vote is the centre of the coin. Each week holders pick which memecoin the next edit covers. One wallet gets one vote, whatever it holds, so a large holder cannot buy the result.

So the list of edits on the page is a direct record of what the holders wanted to see. The first three were picked by the page at the start. Every edit after that is there because of a vote.

A losing pick does not disappear. It carries into the next week's vote with the votes it already had. A coin that keeps coming second is, in effect, waiting its turn.

What is being measured

Five things, all taken from the edits and shown on the record page.

How much each edit cost. How many people saw it. How many followers it brought. What the coin's price and holder count did in the week after it went up. And which kinds of spend keep paying for themselves.

What this does not show

An edit that pulls views is a result. It is real, posted and counted, but it is not proof that the coin is a good buy, and an edit that flops is not proof that it is a bad one. Timing, the algorithm and the rest of the market all shape what comes back.

Nothing here says the coin will go up. The site records what was spent and what happened after, and is careful to call that a record.

The edits also differ. The coin, the editor and the sound behind each one are listed beside it, so the record can be read with that in mind.

Where the coin comes in

The coin is not a ticket and holding it buys no say over the edits, apart from the weekly pick. It is the budget of the page. Every edit is paid for out of its fees, every burn takes it out of the supply, and every boost is reach it paid for.

That means the edits reach the coin directly. Burns reduce the supply. Good edits bring new traders, and new traders bring new fees. None of the eight spends is a sale by the page, so the budget never dumps coins on the market. The only way the treasury spends is in the open.
